## Overview

Many QPs sharing one adapter can all have traffic ready to send at the same moment, and only one shared TX pipeline to send it through. This is the same policy shape [Eviction Policy](/qp/eviction-policy) already worked through for the QP Cache -- LRU, priority-weighted, error-weighted -- asked here of a different scarce resource: not which cached context survives, but whose WQE gets the pipeline's next cycle. Unlike that chapter, this one isn't limited to reasoned analysis alone -- a real vendor's own quoted response to a measured anomaly confirms that shipped RNICs do run some internal scheduling policy, and a separate, independent study confirms directly that congestion control alone does not fix the problem this chapter is about.

## Deep walkthrough

**A vendor's own admission that internal scheduling exists -- not this book's inference.** Kong et al. (NSDI 2023) quote NVIDIA's own response, in the paper's Appendix B, to the paper's measured deregistration-versus-throughput finding: "the deregistration control verbs can cause drastic performance reduction mainly because of the NIC internal QoS scheduling policy. The deregistration control verbs have higher priority than other types of operations and will be scheduled first." This is a specific, vendor-acknowledged confirmation that a shipped RNIC runs *some* internal QoS/priority mechanism arbitrating between different operation types -- not a generic industry assumption this book is applying by analogy, a named vendor's own explanation for a specific measured phenomenon. NVIDIA's stated fix was a firmware-level tuning of that same policy, not a hardware redesign -- indirect evidence that at least part of this mechanism is firmware-adjustable rather than fixed at tape-out, a detail [Hardware-Assisted TX Scheduling Engines](/tx/hardware-assisted-tx-scheduling-engines) picks up directly next.

**Candidate policies, the same shapes [Eviction Policy](/qp/eviction-policy) already named for a different resource.** Round-robin services every QP with ready work in a fixed rotation, regardless of how much each one has to send -- simple, and starvation-free by construction, but blind to the fact that one QP's WQE might be trivial to service and another's might occupy the pipeline far longer, the same "recency isn't the same as importance" blind spot [Eviction Policy](/qp/eviction-policy) already named for pure LRU. Weighted round-robin fixes that blindness by giving each QP a share proportional to an assigned weight rather than a flat turn -- Zhang et al. (Justitia) describe exactly this shape as a working software-layer mitigation, distributing rate tokens "among the active resource-hungry flows in a round-robin fashion," with a stated straightforward extension to weights ("If a flow's weight is `w_i`, Justitia can ensure it receives `w_i`-proportional tokens during each round"). Priority scheduling lets some traffic -- NVIDIA's own deregistration-verbs example above -- jump ahead of ordinary traffic entirely, at the same starvation risk [Multi-QP Error Arbitration](/qp/multi-qp-error-arbitration) already named for priority-weighted fault reporting: a steady stream of high-priority work can, in principle, indefinitely delay something lower-priority sharing the same gate.

**A concrete worked example, tying the fence mechanism back to that starvation risk.** Say QP1 posts a fenced Work Request and stalls waiting on an earlier read to complete ([Single-QP Pipeline Stalls](/tx/single-qp-pipeline-stalls) covers that stall on its own), while QP2 has a continuous stream of ordinary work ready to go. Under strict priority with QP2 favored, QP2 simply gets serviced every turn while QP1 has nothing eligible to offer -- unremarkable so far, since QP1's fence genuinely leaves it with nothing ready to send. The interesting case is what happens next: QP1's fenced read completes, its Work Requests become eligible again, and the fence itself is gone -- but if QP2's priority advantage and continuous ready work haven't changed, QP1 can stay starved indefinitely anyway. The fence explains why QP1 was blocked *before*; the scheduling policy, not the fence, is what explains it staying blocked *after*. Round-robin doesn't share this failure mode -- QP1 simply resumes its normal turn the instant it has eligible work, with no memory of how long it sat idle -- which is exactly the starvation-free-by-construction property named above, and exactly what strict priority trades away in exchange for servicing genuinely urgent work predictably fast.

**A working example -- built specifically because the hardware didn't already do this.** Justitia's own scheduling mechanism is shipped, but it's explicitly a software/driver-layer mitigation, not a description of on-chip scheduling hardware: its shapers are "implemented in the RDMA driver," built as a workaround for RNICs that, at the time of that study, lacked a programmable on-chip packet scheduler at all -- the paper states this directly: "Justitia works on existing RDMA NICs that do not have programmable packet schedulers." It's precisely what it looks like: working evidence for where at least some real deployments have historically landed on the fixed/programmable/software-driven spectrum [Hardware-Assisted TX Scheduling Engines](/tx/hardware-assisted-tx-scheduling-engines) covers in full next, not proof that no RNIC has ever shipped a hardware scheduler.

**Why this is not [Congestion Control Engine](/congestion/)'s question wearing a different label -- confirmed directly, not asserted by this book.** Justitia's authors tested this directly: running their anomaly-reproducing benchmarks with "PFC enabled at both the NICs and switch ports, DCQCN... enabled at the NICs, and ECN markings enabled," they still found "latency- and throughput-sensitive flows still suffer unpredictably." Their own diagnosis, on a topology deliberately simple enough to rule out the network itself as the cause: "the network is not the source of anomalies in these experiments, and thus explains why DCQCN does not fix those anomalies. Rather, at the end hosts, RNICs' immediately processing all ready-to-consume messages to achieve work conservation is very likely to cause head-of-line (HOL) blocking." This is a direct, empirical confirmation -- not this book's own organizational convenience -- that TX-pipeline scheduling and congestion-control rate-limiting are separate problems: DCQCN reacts to network-path congestion signals (ECN marks a switch set), while this chapter's problem is entirely internal to the RNIC, present even when the network itself is uncongested and blameless.

> [!CAUTION] Gotcha
> It's reasonable to assume DCQCN-style congestion control already handles unfairness between QPs, since it's the mechanism this book's future [Congestion Control Engine](/congestion/) owns for reacting to network congestion. It doesn't, confirmed directly rather than assumed: DCQCN and PFC were both active and both irrelevant in the study that measured this failure mode.

**Where this is checked, and what happens next.** The scheduling decision this chapter describes happens at the same gate [Single-QP Pipeline Stalls](/tx/single-qp-pipeline-stalls) already named -- wherever a real pipeline actually holds ready-but-not-yet-serviced WQEs from multiple QPs -- consulting whatever policy (round-robin, weighted, priority) governs that gate. What actually runs that policy decision -- fixed silicon, a programmable engine, or a firmware-adjustable knob like NVIDIA's own QoS mechanism above -- is [Hardware-Assisted TX Scheduling Engines](/tx/hardware-assisted-tx-scheduling-engines)'s subject, immediately next.

> [!TIP] Why
> None of the three policies above is a universally correct choice, and this book isn't picking one for the reader, the same honest position [Eviction Policy](/qp/eviction-policy) took for cache victim selection. Round-robin's fairness is cheap and simple but blind to how much work each QP is actually asking for; weighted round-robin fixes that at the cost of needing an external owner for the weights, the same cost [Eviction Policy](/qp/eviction-policy)'s priority-weighted option paid; strict priority serves genuinely urgent operations (control-plane work like deregistration, per NVIDIA's own example) predictably fast, at a structural starvation risk for whatever it deprioritizes. What this chapter adds beyond that already-familiar tradeoff is the confirmed fact that no amount of network-side congestion control substitutes for getting this policy right -- DCQCN and PFC were both active and both irrelevant in the study that measured this failure mode directly, which means whichever of these three policies (or some blend) a real vendor picks, it's the only lever that actually reaches this specific problem.

[Hardware-Assisted TX Scheduling Engines](/tx/hardware-assisted-tx-scheduling-engines) is next: what actually runs whichever policy this chapter names -- fixed silicon, a programmable engine, or firmware.

## References

- No IETF or IBTA standard governs internal multi-QP TX scheduling policy -- it is implementation-specific silicon and firmware design, not a wire-format or software-interface contract any standards body defines. This chapter's treatment is grounded in the sources cited in-text above, not a standards document.
